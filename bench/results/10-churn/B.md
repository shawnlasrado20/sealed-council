I’d test changes that reduce effort and waste before cutting prices. “Too expensive” may mean customers aren’t getting enough value from the meals they actually cook; exit surveys suggest hypotheses, not causes.

1. **Offer a genuinely low-effort first box.** Let new customers choose meals requiring ≤15 minutes, with fewer steps and pre-prepped ingredients. Keep the price unchanged so the test isolates convenience.
   - Randomize new customers between the current experience and the low-effort option.
   - **Primary measure:** first-to-second-box paid retention, against the current 82%.
   - **Supporting measures:** meals cooked, reported cooking effort, and retention through box three.
   - **Guardrails:** contribution margin per acquired customer, food satisfaction, and fulfillment errors.

2. **Make the next box fit actual usage.** Before the second shipment, offer a clear choice to reduce meal count, delay delivery, or keep the current box. Show the resulting total price. This tests whether excess food and commitment drive both waste and perceived expense.
   - Randomize separately from the first test, or use a factorial design if traffic supports it.
   - **Primary measure:** cumulative contribution margin per acquired customer over 90 days.
   - **Supporting measures:** active paying customers at day 90, boxes purchased, and reported food waste.
   - Track skips separately from cancellations; a delayed cancellation is not necessarily better retention.

**Avoid:** blanket discounts, forced cancellation friction, bundling both changes into one treatment, and declaring success from survey responses or second-box retention alone. Predefine a worthwhile improvement and sample size; follow cohorts long enough to detect whether churn merely shifts later.

Skipped: a price-cut test until usage and margin evidence justify it. Risk: both changes could improve retention while reducing profit, so measure customer-level economics.
