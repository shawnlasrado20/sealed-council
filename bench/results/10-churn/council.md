**1. A low-effort first box at the same price.** New customers choose from meals that take 15 minutes or less, with fewer steps and pre-prepped ingredients. Keep the price unchanged so the test measures convenience alone and isn't mixed up with a discount. Early cooking friction may also be behind "too expensive": the meals didn't feel worth the money.
- **Primary metric:** paid conversion from box 1 to box 2, compared with today's 82%.
- **Supporting metrics:** meals actually cooked, reported effort, and retention through box 4.
- **Guardrails:** contribution margin per customer, food ratings, and fulfillment errors.

**2. Make the next box fit actual use.** Before the second shipment, offer three clear choices: fewer meals or servings, a delayed delivery, or keep the current box. Show the new total for each. This tests whether too much food and too much commitment are causing both the spoilage and the feeling that it's too expensive.
- **Primary metric:** cumulative contribution margin per acquired customer over 90 days. This stops a cheaper box from looking like a win while profit falls.
- **Supporting metrics:** active paying customers at day 90, boxes bought, reported waste, and how many customers move back up to a larger box.
- Track skips separately from cancellations. A skip can just be a delayed cancel.

**Test design:** randomize new cohorts, running each test separately or as a 2x2 if traffic allows. Set the minimum worthwhile lift and the sample size in advance. Let cohorts run 8 to 12 weeks so you can see whether churn just moves later. Break results down by acquisition channel, because customers who joined on a discount churn differently.

**Avoid:**
- Blanket price cuts or bigger first-box discounts. They attract deal-seekers and erode margin.
- Treating exit surveys as causes. They are hypotheses, so check them against behavior.
- Making cancellation harder on purpose. Offering pause or downsize is fine.
- Bundling several changes into one treatment.
- Starting with a freshness overhaul. Right-sizing the box tests the waste problem first, and spoilage complaints can be watched as a guardrail.
