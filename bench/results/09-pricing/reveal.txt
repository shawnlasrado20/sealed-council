rank claude:
RANK: B > A

B is more concrete and more decisive, which is what the brief asked for. It commits to one offer and spells out a sequence of tests with clear pass and fail thresholds. Two details make it more useful in practice: it judges the split test on cash collected rather than signups, and it handles hosted processing costs with a usage cap. It also gives a clear signal for when to add the team plan. Its weaknesses: it states some guesses as fact (for example, that a monthly plan "trains them to cancel"), its 50-user, two-week split is too small to be reliable, and it seems to run slightly over the 300-word limit.

A is careful and honest about uncertainty. Its points that the decision rule is not statistically reliable, and that 12 buyers at $8 is only $96 a month, are good. But it hedges too much. Its recommended price makes little money, and its main test only checks the 12 interested users without comparing prices. As a result it is less useful for actually deciding on a price.
rank agent:
RANK: A > C

A is the better launch plan. It picks one price from the brief, treats the 12 “I’ll pay” signals as a hypothesis, and tests with a real checkout plus a kill rule: four purchases in seven days. It also tracks the right follow-ups—completed docs, use in more than one week, second-month renewal, and processing cost—so a burst of first-month buys that then cancel is not mistaken for a business. Other prices are gated on evidence: one-time only if use is occasional and ongoing cost is covered; team only after shared workflows and a company budget show up; usage-based only after costs actually vary.

C is more ambitious and less disciplined. It invents prices and a yearly option the brief did not offer, then contradicts itself by surveying willingness to pay before setting a price while also sending a checkout the same week. “Too cheap / toy” is asserted, not measured. A team workspace, branding, and watermarks assume a product shape this CLI has not earned, and an A/B on hundreds of free users will be noise. The anti–one-time and anti–usage-based instincts are fair, but they do not make up for overbuilding and a muddled first test.

Its weaknesses matter: assumptions about cancellation are unsupported, low conversion cannot identify packaging or trust as the cause, and two-week cash collected unfairly favors upfront payment over recurring revenue. An unlimited add-on also fails to bound costs.

Asking hypothetical willingness to pay adds less evidence than requesting payment. Claims that low prices signal poor quality and one-time payments undermine sustainability are too categorical.

Neither establishes usage frequency or delivery costs, which could change the recommendation.
